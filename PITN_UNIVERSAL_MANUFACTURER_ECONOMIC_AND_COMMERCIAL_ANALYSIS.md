# EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

POWER IN THE NUMBERS (PITN) establishes a persistent digital identity and blockchain/Web3 namespace infrastructure designed to connect physical robotics components to the information that already describes them.

Robotics manufacturers operate across complex information environments involving engineering, procurement, sourcing, manufacturing, quality management, inventory, maintenance, suppliers, and lifecycle operations. Component information may already exist across ERP, PLM, MES, CAD, PDM, QMS, warehouse, inventory, and maintenance systems. However, identifying the correct records, reconciling different identifiers, retrieving authoritative technical information, and maintaining relationships between components and assemblies can require repeated manual effort.

PITN addresses this information-connection problem through persistent identity references, namespace classification, and relationships between physical components, product definitions, assemblies, and associated information.

Its purpose is not to replace existing manufacturing software. It is to establish an identity and information-connection foundation that complements existing enterprise systems while preserving their source authority.

The manufacturer opportunity extends beyond operational information retrieval.

PITN's blockchain/Web3 namespace architecture introduces additional economic and commercial considerations, including:

- Persistent component identity registration.
- Manufacturer participation in designated component namespaces, subject to applicable rights and agreements.
- Registration pricing and eligible namespace-owner revenue participation.
- Blockchain mint transaction evidence associated with registered identities.
- Component information continuity across suppliers, assemblies, and lifecycle operations.
- Potential downstream commercialization through authorized licensing and sublicensing.
- Measurable reductions in repetitive information-related work.

This publication integrates three previously separate economic and technical analyses into one manufacturer-facing framework.

It establishes five distinct financial categories:

1. Existing manufacturing software expenditure.
2. Component identity registration acquisition costs and eligible owner returns.
3. Potential recurring information-work labor savings.
4. Implementation, licensing, integration, and operating expenses.
5. Potential downstream commercial licensing revenue.

These categories must be evaluated independently before calculating a manufacturer's overall economic position.

# 2. THE ROBOTICS MANUFACTURING INFORMATION PROBLEM

Robotics manufacturers manage extensive populations of physical components, assemblies, subsystems, and completed robotic systems.

A humanoid robot may incorporate:

- Frames, structural members, housings, and mechanical assemblies.
- Motors, actuators, reducers, gears, and robotic joints.
- Bearings, fasteners, connectors, pins, and cable harnesses.
- Encoders, force sensors, torque sensors, IMUs, and other sensing devices.
- Controllers, circuit boards, embedded electronics, and power-conversion equipment.
- Battery cells, modules, power-distribution assemblies, and charging systems.
- Cameras, depth sensors, vision systems, and perception modules.
- End effectors, grippers, robotic fingers, and tool-changing mechanisms.

Each physical component or product definition may have multiple associated identifiers and records:

- Manufacturer Part Number (MPN).
- Supplier Stock Keeping Unit (SKU).
- Internal manufacturer part number.
- Engineering part number.
- Revision and configuration identifiers.
- Lot, batch, and serial numbers.
- Purchase orders and receiving references.
- Technical specifications and datasheets.
- CAD models and engineering drawings.
- Certifications, inspections, and test records.
- Supplier documentation.
- BOM positions and parent assemblies.
- Installation and maintenance records.
- Replacement and lifecycle history.

These records frequently exist across different systems, databases, supplier portals, spreadsheets, and document repositories.

The information-connection problem is determining:

- Which records refer to the same component or product definition.
- Which identifier is original and which is internally assigned.
- Which revision or configuration is applicable.
- Which source is authoritative.
- Which BOM position and assembly relationships are correct.
- Which supplier and technical references apply.
- Which physical instance is installed in a particular robot.
- Which service and lifecycle records belong to that instance.

PITN addresses the effort required to establish and maintain these relationships.

# 3. PERSISTENT IDENTITY AND BLOCKCHAIN/WEB3 NAMESPACE ARCHITECTURE

PITN uses blockchain/Web3 namespace architecture as a classification and persistent digital naming foundation for physical component identity.

Its namespace architecture is intended to provide organized identity categories for robotics components and systems.

Examples include:

- .robotsystem
- .roboticarm
- .roboticleg
- .robotichand
- .roboticwrist
- .actuator
- .reducer
- .encoder
- .robotsensor
- .controlsystem
- .robotcontroller
- .batterycell
- .visionmodule
- .endeffector
- .toolchanger

The broader PITN namespace architecture is structured around 34 robotics-related categories.

Namespace classification is intended to support consistent naming and identity organization across component families and the robotics manufacturing ecosystem.

## 3.1 Four distinct identity elements

The following elements must remain distinguishable:

**Namespace:** The blockchain/Web3 classification and naming domain.

**Registered digital name:** A name registered within a namespace under the applicable registration and provider rules.

**Component identity record:** Structured information connecting a product definition or physical instance to identifiers, references, and relationships.

**Physical component:** The actual manufactured, purchased, received, installed, serviced, or recovered item.

A namespace registration is not, by itself, a complete component record, proof of physical authenticity, technical verification, certification, or compatibility.

## 3.2 Preservation of existing manufacturer identifiers

PITN does not require manufacturers to abandon existing identifiers.

Original MPNs, SKUs, engineering numbers, revisions, serial numbers, and supplier identifiers remain in place and can be associated with persistent identity references.

Existing source systems retain authority over the records and business processes they control.

The intended information relationship is:

PHYSICAL COMPONENT

↓

PITN PERSISTENT IDENTITY AND NAMESPACE CLASSIFICATION

↓

ORIGINAL IDENTIFIERS AND APPROVED RELATIONSHIPS

↓

AUTHORIZED TECHNICAL, BOM, SUPPLIER, ENGINEERING, QUALITY AND LIFECYCLE REFERENCES

↓

EXISTING MANUFACTURER INFORMATION SYSTEMS

ERP / PLM / MES / CAD / PDM / QMS / WMS / CMMS / SUPPLIER SYSTEMS

PITN connects authorized references and relationships. It does not automatically copy, validate, replace, or take authority over source records.

## 3.3 Manufacturer namespace participation

A manufacturer may evaluate participation in a designated component namespace, subject to the applicable ownership, registration, provider, and commercial agreements.

Namespace control, legal ownership, technical administration, registration pricing authority, and revenue entitlement are distinct rights.

The exact rights must be established by the governing agreements.

A namespace registration does not transfer ownership of a physical component, manufacturer trademark, or underlying intellectual property.

## 7.1 Registration pricing assumptions

The supplied PITN registration material identifies:

- Illustrative listed registration price: $5.00.
- Promotional discounts at qualifying purchase prices.
- A described 50/50 revenue-sharing arrangement for eligible namespace owners.
- A PITN-reported historical average purchase price of approximately $1.75.

The $1.75 figure is a PITN-reported historical average from supplied material. It is not an independently verified market-wide blockchain/Web3 registration average.

The $5.00 price and discount scenarios are illustrative, not universal registration prices.

## 7.2 Registration price and owner-return calculations

Actual Purchase Price = Listed Price × (1 − Discount Rate)

Illustrative Owner Return = Actual Purchase Price × 50%

Consolidated Net Economic Cost = Actual Purchase Price − Eligible Owner Return

| Discount | Listed price | Purchaser payment | Eligible 50% owner return | Net cost if purchaser is eligible owner |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0% | $5.00 | $5.00 | $2.50 | $2.50 |
| 20% | $5.00 | $4.00 | $2.00 | $2.00 |
| 40% | $5.00 | $3.00 | $1.50 | $1.50 |
| 50% | $5.00 | $2.50 | $1.25 | $1.25 |
| 70% | $5.00 | $1.50 | $0.75 | $0.75 |
| 90% | $5.00 | $0.50 | $0.25 | $0.25 |
| Historical average | — | $1.75 | $0.875 | $0.875 |

The historical-average row applies the described 50% share as an illustrative calculation. It does not establish that every historical registration qualified.

## 7.3 Essential distinction between payment and owner revenue

Using the 50% promotional discount example:

Listed registration price: $5.00.

Promotional discount: 50%.

Actual purchaser payment: $2.50.

Illustrative owner return: $1.25.

The purchaser pays $2.50. The eligible owner separately receives $1.25 under the assumed revenue-sharing arrangement.

The $1.25 is not a checkout discount.

Where the purchaser and eligible namespace owner are the same party, the consolidated economic cost may be calculated as:

$2.50 − $1.25 = $1.25.

Where the purchaser and owner are different parties, their financial positions must be calculated separately.

A manufacturer cannot automatically deduct an owner return from registration expenditure unless it is contractually entitled to receive that return.

Actual eligibility, revenue calculations, fees, deductions, taxes, payment timing, and provider conditions must be verified before financial commitments are made.

# 10. REGISTRATION RECORDS AND BLOCKCHAIN MINT TRANSACTION EVIDENCE

PITN distinguishes between operational registration information and blockchain transaction evidence.

These records are complementary but serve different purposes.

## 10.1 Operational registration record

The registration platform may maintain fields such as:

- Registration name.
- Namespace or TLD.
- Registration status.
- Ownership information.
- Pricing information.

The operational record answers:

What identity is registered, under which namespace, and what does the registration platform report about its status and ownership?

The record depends on the platform's implementation and data-maintenance practices.

## 10.2 Blockchain mint transaction record

The mint transaction may provide:

- Transaction hash.
- Transaction timestamp.
- Block number, where available.
- Mint input data.
- Relevant transaction and network references.

The blockchain transaction answers:

What transaction was submitted to the blockchain, and what transaction evidence can be independently inspected on the applicable network?

The transaction hash provides a reference to a blockchain transaction. Timestamp and block information provide additional context, subject to the network and available transaction data.

Mint input data may identify information submitted as part of the transaction, depending on the implementation and encoding.

## 10.3 Relationship between the records

Where supported by the implementation, the operational registration record should retain a reference to its associated blockchain transaction.

This relationship can allow users to navigate from a registration record to the relevant mint transaction evidence.

The records must remain distinguishable.

An active dashboard registration does not independently establish every blockchain transaction attribute. A transaction hash does not independently establish the operational status of a registration.

Neither record proves the physical authenticity of a component, its manufacturer origin, its technical specifications, or its certification.

## 10.4 Physical-world verification

A digital identity must be associated with the physical object through appropriate procedures.

A manufacturer may need to establish:

- Who created the identity.
- Which organization is authoritative for the product definition.
- Which physical component was assigned the identity.
- How the identity is applied or attached.
- How the identifier is read or inspected.
- How the component relates to its lot or serial number.
- How installation in a robot or subsystem is recorded.
- How replacements, maintenance, and lifecycle events are maintained.

Blockchain immutability does not independently guarantee the truth of information submitted to a blockchain.

Physical verification, source authority, assignment procedures, access controls, and governance remain necessary.

# 16. MANUFACTURER ROI AND FINANCIAL VALIDATION

The manufacturer should calculate financial benefits using observed workflow performance and complete adoption costs.

Annual Task Labor Cost:

Annual Task Volume × Average Task Minutes ÷ 60 × Loaded Hourly Labor Rate

Annual Gross Labor Savings:

Baseline Annual Task Labor Cost − Post-Implementation Annual Task Labor Cost

Annual Net Benefit:

Measured Gross Annual Savings − Incremental Annual Operating Costs

First-Year Net Benefit:

Measured Gross Annual Savings − Initial Implementation Costs − First-Year Operating Costs

Simple Payback Period:

Initial Implementation Costs ÷ Average Monthly Net Benefit

Payback should only be calculated when average monthly net benefit is positive.

Incremental costs must include applicable registration, licensing, implementation, integration, configuration, training, security review, infrastructure, support, maintenance, and internal labor.

The manufacturer must avoid counting the same time reduction twice.

Quality improvements, reduced downtime, inventory improvements, scrap reductions, throughput gains, and warranty improvements must be separately measured before being monetized.

# 17. CONTROLLED MANUFACTURER PILOT AND VALIDATION FRAMEWORK

A controlled pilot is the recommended method for establishing whether PITN delivers measurable information-cost reductions.

The manufacturer may select a representative component family such as actuators, reducers, encoders, sensors, connectors, or structural components.

## 17.1 Pilot stages

**STEP 1 — Define scope**

Select the component family, robot platform, participating departments, relevant systems, and measurable workflows.

**STEP 2 — Capture baseline**

Measure task volumes, completion times, searches, errors, unsuccessful retrievals, and loaded labor rates before implementation.

**STEP 3 — Map existing identifiers**

Map MPNs, SKUs, internal part numbers, revisions, lot/serial references, and source records. Preserve original identifiers and source authority.

**STEP 4 — Establish identity relationships**

Create or associate persistent identity references with manufacturer-approved records, BOM positions, assemblies, supplier references, technical documents, and lifecycle records where authorized.

**STEP 5 — Configure implementation**

Define the actual systems, interfaces, data, permissions, integration methods, and functions included in the deployment.

Do not assume an unconfigured enterprise integration already exists.

**STEP 6 — Train and test**

Use controlled test cases covering typical workflows, duplicate records, revision conflicts, incomplete records, exceptions, and unsuccessful retrievals.

**STEP 7 — Measure post-implementation performance**

Repeat comparable tasks and measure time, accuracy, successful retrieval, errors, exceptions, and repeated work.

A faster but incorrect retrieval is not a legitimate saving.

**STEP 8 — Calculate financial results**

Calculate observed gross savings and deduct actual implementation and recurring operating costs.

**STEP 9 — Review and decide**

Document results, limitations, acceptance criteria, data quality issues, and whether further deployment is justified.
